Lesson A1 — The Big Picture: What Is an Experience Study?

The story. A life insurance company sells a promise: "Pay us premiums, and we'll pay out if something happens." To price that promise, the company makes guesses — e.g., "of 1,000 policies like this, about 2 will lapse (stop paying) this year." An experience study is the company checking its homework: it looks back at real policies and asks, "Did reality match our guesses?"
What one row is. A row is not a person and not the whole life of a policy. It is one policy observed during one time slice. Look at your rows: each has tranche_days = 220 or 37 — a window of days. So row ...101 says roughly: "Policy 81037900101 contributed 220 days of observation in this slice." (ASSUMPTION: each policy appears only once in your extract — confirm this with the data owner.)
Why the same policy can appear in several rows. Think of a policy's life as being chopped into slices, like a video chopped into frames:

Each slice becomes its own row because things change over time — the policyholder gets older, premiums change, the policy might lapse. Slicing keeps everything constant within a row, which is what the math in later lessons needs. Your 10 rows are tiny slices of many longer lives — some rows show policy_duration = 4, meaning that policy was already 4 years old when this slice was recorded.

Lesson B1 — Why Survival Analysis Exists (Why Your Rows Break Ordinary Methods)

The problem in one sentence. In your 10 rows, only 2 rows (…108, …109) show active = 0 — some kind of "stopped" outcome. The other 8 policies are simply still going when the observation window closes. Ordinary statistics forces you to label every row as "event" or "no event," and neither label is honest for those 8.
Analogy. You watch 10 runners for 5 minutes. Two cross the finish line; eight are still running when your stopwatch battery dies. If someone asks "how many runners finished?", the honest answer is "2 so far — the other 8 are unknown, not 'never.'" Calling them "did not finish" is a lie; calling them "finished" is also a lie. They are censored: we only know their outcome is later than what we saw.
plain
Copy
time →
runner 1:  |----X  (event: finished, time known)
runner 2:  |----X  (event)
runner 3:  |-----o (censored: still running at cutoff)
...
cutoff:          ↑ we stop watching here
Why plain logistic regression fails. Logistic regression needs every row to have a definite 0/1 outcome. Your 8 censored rows have no definite outcome — the event may happen next month, next year, or never. Coding censored rows as 0 (no event) teaches the model "these people never lapse," which understates risk; coding them as 1 is equally false. So the model trains on labels that don't exist.
Why plain time regression fails. You could try regressing "time until stopping" — but for censored rows you don't have the true time; you only have a lower bound (T > 37 or 220 days). Feeding 37 in as if it were the real time biases everything downward.
Python snippet — see the trap concretely:
Python
Copy
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression
import pandas as pd

df = pd.read_csv("your_file.csv")
y = 1 - df["active"]          # 1 = "stopped", 0 = still active
print(y.value_counts())       # 2 ones, 8 zeros ← but the 8 aren't true zeros!
model = LogisticRegression().fit(df[["policy_holder_age"]], y)  # trained on fake labels
Summary: Survival analysis exists because censored rows carry "T is at least this big" information that yes/no or plain time methods can't use without lying.

Yes, you can compare them — but only with the right ruler, and your 10 rows will expose the catch immediately. Let me walk through it.
The comparison trap. Logistic regression answers: "What's the probability this policy stops by the end of the study window?" — one fixed snapshot. Survival analysis answers: "What's the probability it stops by any time t you ask about?" — a whole curve of snapshots. Comparing a single snapshot to a full curve is like comparing a photo of a race at the 5-minute mark to a full video: the photo can't tell you who was ahead at minute 3.
So how do you compare fairly? Pin the survival model to the same fixed horizon as the classifier, then ask both: "P(event before day 220)?" Now both give one number per policy, and you can score them on held-out rows. That's legitimate.
But here's the catch — three of them, actually:
  1. Your logistic model was trained on fake labels (censored rows coded as 0). Garbage in. To compare fairly you'd need to retrain logistic on only rows whose true outcome is known by day 220 — in your data, that means training on a handful of rows. Which brings us to…
  2. Your 10 rows can't support any comparison. With 2 events total, splitting into train/test gives you nothing stable. Any "Model A beats Model B" result would be noise. This isn't a method problem; it's a data problem.
  3. Even trained well, the logistic model throws away information — it ignores when the event happened and how long censored policies were watched. Survival models use all of it, so with enough data they almost always win on these tasks.
The "better method" you're sensing: it's not a third model family — it's censoring-aware scoring rules. The standard trio (we'll cover them in Lesson B11):
plain
Copy
C-index        → "how well does it rank policies: riskiest first?"
Time-dep. AUC  → "how well does it separate event vs non-event by day t?"
Brier score    → "how close are the predicted probabilities to what actually happened?"
These metrics know what censoring is, so a model can't cheat by mislabeling censored rows. Ordinary accuracy/AUC from classification is blind to censoring and will happily reward the garbage-label logistic model.
Bottom line: classification vs survival is not a fair fight on survival-type questions — logistic lacks the vocabulary (time + censoring). Compare survival models against each other using C-index/Brier score, once you have enough events.
